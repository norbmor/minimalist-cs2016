Collins & Stabler (2016) give an axiomatization of minimalist syntax: forty-four definitions, five Chain-based variants and fourteen theorems, precise enough that one can ask mechanically whether a sequence of operations is a derivation. We transcribe the axiomatization into Python, one named function per definition and theorem, and wrap it in a driver that refuses any step the definitions do not license, so that a derivation object is a certificate rather than a transcript. Seventeen derivations validate against the definition of a derivation and against Theorems 5-10, and 76 automated checks pass. The implementation reproduces the paper's argument against the "plug-back-in" model of Transfer: remnant movement inside one phase is spelled out correctly, but the paper's two-phase example (21) is pronounced "how likely John to win you think that John is", and that derivation still converges. An implementation of the paper's Section 12 alternative pronounces it correctly. We also report three results the paper does not state. Theorem 4, the correspondence between token-based and Chain-based objects, fails on exhaustively enumerated fragments from three Merge nodes on, under every reading of its misprinted locality condition. The Section 12 sketch works under one reading of "W minus those already transferred" and makes successive-cyclic movement underivable under the other. And with the unordered trigger features the paper assumes, adding selection to Triggered Merge still admits the [TP vP [T' T DP]] orders the paper leaves to other constraints.
